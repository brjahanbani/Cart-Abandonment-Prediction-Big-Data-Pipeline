The Research Spine: Two Tracks

AUC 0.9947 → 0.63 once leakage is removed

One intellectual core, two outputs. The question in both is identical: what was knowable at time t? Offline, that question is point-in-time correctness. In a running system, it is event time and watermarks.

Track 1 — Primary (MSc thesis)

ACTIVE

An open e-commerce burst workload for elastic stateful streaming. Nexmark — the synthetic auction benchmark the field evaluates on — measures steady-state throughput. The benefits claimed for disaggregated state in Flink 2.0 are realised during disruption: bursts, rescaling, recovery. Nexmark generates none of them. Deliverable: a generator, not a dataset.

Track 2 — Closing (papers)

CLOSE BY DEC

The leakage audit, finished and shipped. Paper 1 accepted at IJACSA (Oct 2026); arXiv preprint posted 21 Sep. Paper 2 reframed as point-in-time correctness in feature pipelines — as-of join vs latest-value join over item-property snapshots — targeting a 4-page DEEM@SIGMOD short paper. Cart abandonment becomes the case study, not the subject.

What the first paper found

Three leakage mechanisms across 43,917 cart sessions: arithmetic target reconstruction, endpoint timing contamination, and label ambiguity affecting 4.0% of sessions. Completed-session features reach 0.9947 AUC. Rebuilt at decision time, performance falls to roughly 0.63–0.65 at the first cutoff and 0.55–0.61 thereafter. The honest number is worse than the headline — which is what makes it a finding.

Why it points at KTH

A watermark is a formal, machine-checkable statement of what was knowable at time t — the apparatus streaming systems built for exactly this problem, and that feature pipelines lack. That single idea connects Carbone (event-time semantics, state, Flink) and Dowling (point-in-time correct feature joins, Hopsworks) into one research identity instead of two unrelated papers.

How an AUC of 0.997 became a research identity

Six chapters, one question asked over and over: what was knowable at time t?

A course project produced a near-perfect score. Taking it apart became the first paper; rebuilding it honestly became the method; the method points straight at the thesis. Every chapter left an artifact in the repo.

  1. Prologue · spring 2026

    A course proposal

    Big Data & Analytics with Prof. Sayar. The brief: predict whether a shopper who adds something to the cart will buy it, using the public Retail Rocket log — 2.76 million clicks over 137 days.

  2. I

    The Build

    15 – 17 Jun 2026

    Clicks grouped into 30-minute sessions, bots removed, eight session features computed. A live pipeline — Kafka → Spark → MongoDB → LSTM → dashboard — scores sessions as they stream in.

    Lesson · a streaming system is not automatically a point-in-time system. The producer sent pre-computed session keys.

    5-Pipeline/ stage1_data_cleaning_feature_engineering.py
  3. II

    The Score That Was Too Good

    Jun – Jul 2026

    ROC-AUC 0.9969, then 0.9946 on cart sessions. Real shoppers are never that predictable. A first fix — timing recomputed without the purchase event — dropped it to 0.65–0.69, but nobody yet knew exactly why.

    Lesson · an implausibly good score is a question, not a result.

    offline_cart_abandonment_prediction.py train_xgboost_baseline.py
  4. III

    The Audit

    28 – 29 Jul 2026

    Every feature traced back to the raw clicks. Three culprits:

    1 · The hidden answer

    total − views − carts = purchases. The model just subtracted.

    2 · The peek at the end

    Session age and cart recency were measured at the purchase itself.

    3 · The fuzzy label

    "Bought A, abandoned B" counted as a purchase.

    Remove them and the score falls to ~0.61. The missing analysis script was rebuilt (19 of 20 cells reproduce), every number got a provenance row, and a decision-time dataset was built: features from only what had happened by each moment.

    Lesson · the score was never measuring the task. Honest prediction at decision time sits around 0.55–0.66.

    reanalysis_controlled_audit.py build_prefix_dataset.py docs/results_provenance.md
  5. IV

    Into the World

    Aug – Sep 2026

    The audit became "When Offline Performance Misleads". Submitted to IJACSA (MS-17-10-0048) and posted to arXiv by Prof. Sayar on 21 Sep (cs.AI). Accepted in October. The RecSys route was dropped, and a separate thesis track opened: stream processing, KTH.

    Lesson · describe the finding, not the venue — 0.995 collapsing to 0.61 is the sentence people remember.

    paper/ijacsa/ paper/arxiv/
  6. V

    The Return

    29 Sep – 1 Oct 2026

    The paper source moved into the repo. One script now rebuilds the paper's data table from the raw clicks — identical to the last byte — and recomputes every number the paper quotes: 27 of 29 match. The audit, turned on its own paper, found one slip: the fuzzy-label count is 1,722 (3.9%), not 1,745 (4.0%) — 23 sessions counted twice.

    Lesson · reproducibility is not a chore at the end; it is how you find your own mistakes first.

    build_cart_session_table.py completed-session__descriptive-stats.json
  7. VI

    The Fork Now

    2 Oct 2026 →

    First experiments for paper 2 (DEEM@SIGMOD). Freeze time at one moment — the shopper has just put the first item in the cart — and ask: will this visit end in a purchase? Then add the shopper's history, counted at the right moment or the wrong one.

    Step 1 · what they did so far

    0.664

    Clicks, time spent, items viewed. The honest starting point.

    Step 2 · + history up to now ✓

    0.711

    Their past purchases and the product's past sales, counted before today. Real, honest gain.

    Step 3 · + history over all data ✗

    0.977

    The same two facts counted over the whole dataset — including today. Not a better model: an accidental cheat.

    Why step 3 cheats — two first-time shoppers

    Shopper A
    buys today    		Shopper B
    leaves today
    Past purchases, counted up to now ✓00
    Past purchases, counted over all the data ✗10

    The wrong count includes today's purchase — the very thing being predicted. "1" simply means "buys today", so the model reads the answer off the column. Same trick as paper 1 (purchases = total − views − carts), arriving through an ordinary "customer history" table.

    How much each timing mistake inflates the score (extra AUC over doing it correctly)

    Product info from the end of the data+0.000 · harmless
    Product info from the nearest week (may be after today)+0.008 · tiny
    History over future days, today left out+0.015 · small
    History over everything, today included+0.266 · catastrophic

    Step 3 − Step 2 = 0.977 − 0.711 = +0.266. Product info = weekly price/stock snapshots. All at the first add-to-cart, sessions split by date (train on earlier, test on later), XGBoost. First quick experiments — to be confirmed before the paper.

    The message · not all timing mistakes are equal. Some do nothing, some a little — and one quietly turns an honest 0.71 into a fake 0.98.

    Open decision · keep paper 2 on product-snapshot joins, or widen it to which point-in-time violations actually matter (recommended).

    6-Point-In-Time/probes/ docs/point_in_time_log.md

The throughline

Paper 1 asked it of one table: which columns knew the future?

Paper 2 asks it of feature pipelines: which joins and aggregates cross the line, and by how much?

The thesis asks it of streaming systems, where watermarks make "knowable at time t" a guarantee — under bursts, rescaling and failure.
